Case Study: Gymshark and Customer Insight

Reading focus: Resourcefulness and customer insight

The opening. In the early 2010s, Ben Francis was a university student in Birmingham, England. He delivered pizzas, studied, and spent much of his free time lifting weights with friends. He wanted to enter the fitness industry, so he tried building workout apps and a fitness social network. Those projects attracted some users, but they did not become sustainable businesses.

The first test. Francis started Gymshark in 2012 as a website with very little money and no inventory. The site first used drop shipping: when a customer placed an order, another retailer shipped the product, and Gymshark earned a small margin. The first order took about two months. This approach was limited, but it let the team test whether customers would buy through the site before spending heavily on stock.

The customer problem. The team soon noticed that existing gym clothing did not match what they wanted to wear. Many items felt baggy, rigid, or designed for a different type of lifter. Because Francis and his friends were members of the lifting community, they experienced the frustration themselves and understood the style, fit, and function their peers cared about. They began making fitted, lightweight clothing with a sewing machine and screen printer. Francis helped sew, print, pack, and deliver the early products.

The growth choice. Gymshark sent some early products to fitness creators the team already followed. Those creators wore and shared the clothing with their own communities. In 2013, Gymshark also took a major risk by reserving a small space at the BodyPower fitness expo. The event brought athletes, fans, and the company together. A newly developed fitted tracksuit gained strong attention, and online demand rose sharply after the expo.

The entrepreneurial lesson. Gymshark did not begin with a giant inventory or a perfect product. The team started with accessible resources, learned from being part of the customer community, and changed its offer when the original clothing did not meet the need. Their firsthand experience was valuable insight, but it was not the same as complete evidence. Orders, creator responses, event reactions, and later product results helped test whether other customers agreed.

Source note: Adapted for class from the NEIA Entrepreneur Hub Case 7 reading focus and Gymshark’s official story.

Bridge to the Studio

Gymshark had to make its product understandable and useful to a specific community. Your rebus has a similar challenge: a solver should struggle productively, notice your clues, and eventually understand the idea. Confusion is evidence. Use it to revise.

Rebus Studio Challenge

A rebus represents a word or phrase through position, size, direction, repetition, spacing, or combinations of words and symbols. Make each solution discoverable without simply writing the answer in the puzzle.

Your Deliverable

Create three original entrepreneurial rebuses: a mindset trait, an entrepreneurial action, and a course concept or original phrase. Include a separate answer for each, record a peer test, and revise at least one puzzle.
