Key Terms

Assumption

Something you believe is true before you collect evidence.

Audience

The specific group of people your idea is meant to help.

Idea

A possible product, service, or experience you could create.

Evidence

Information from real people or observations that helps you decide.

The School Snack Cart Pivot

Seventh graders plan to sell healthy snacks after school. They assume students will buy kale chips. Before buying inventory, they offer samples and ask classmates for feedback. Most students prefer baked apple crisps and fruit smoothies.

3

Choose an Idea and Audience

Pick a school-relevant product, service, or experience. Your audience must be more specific than “everyone” or “all students.” Ideas: an after-school snack, organizer, homework planning service, lunchtime activity, school merchandise, or another teacher-approved idea.

4

Build a Five-Question Survey

Write one question of each type. Ask without pushing people toward the answer you want.

Example: What do you currently do when you need a snack after school?

Example: How often do you stay at school after dismissal?

Example: Which matters most in a snack: price, taste, health, or convenience?

Example: Tell us about the last time you wanted a snack after school.

Example: Which of these snack options would you be most likely to buy?

Survey Quality Check

Exchange questions with another pair. Mark questions N (neutral), L (leading), or U (unclear). Revise at least one.

5

Rapid Research

Survey four to six classmates. Record what each person actually says. Do not include names, argue, or try to sell your idea. Your job is to learn.
