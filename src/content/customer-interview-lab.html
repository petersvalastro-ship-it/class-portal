02 / READ

The Calendly Case

Reading focus · Customer insight, questioning assumptions, and perseverance

The recurring frustration. Before founding Calendly, Tope Awotona worked in enterprise software sales. His work depended on conversations and meetings, but arranging those meetings often required repeated messages about available times. Time spent coordinating was time not spent on the purpose of the meeting.

The focused problem. Awotona founded Calendly in 2013 with a vision of simplifying scheduling and reducing the back-and-forth. A person could share availability so another person could choose a workable time. The insight: when finding time to meet is easier, useful connections can happen sooner.

The calculated commitment. Calendly’s official profile says Awotona invested his personal savings in the venture. That shows perseverance and calculated risk, but commitment alone does not prove customers need a product. A founder still needs evidence about who experiences a problem, how often it occurs, what people do now, and whether it matters enough to change behavior.

The customer evidence question. Awotona’s experience was a starting observation. Instead of “Would you use an easier scheduling tool?”, ask: “Tell me about the last time you tried to schedule a meeting with several people.” The second question invites a story about behavior, workarounds, delays, and priorities.

The continuing lesson. Calendly now serves individuals, teams, and organizations whose work depends on meetings. The lesson is not that every problem needs an app. Investigating a focused problem helps reveal what should be built, changed, or left alone.

03 / PRACTICE

Question Lab

Strong questions ask about a specific past experience. They do not advertise an idea or pressure someone toward a preferred answer.

“Would you use an app that makes group work easier?”

Try: “Tell me about the last group project you completed.”

“Don’t deadlines get confusing?”

Try: “How did you keep track of deadlines last week?”

“What features do you want?”

Try: “Where did the process slow down or become difficult?”

The Question Rules

Begin with “Tell me about the last time…” or “Walk me through what happened.” Ask one question at a time. Pause. Follow up with “What happened next?” or “Why did that matter?” Do not collect names, contact information, or sensitive details.

04 / INTERVIEW

Prepare & Interview

Choose one school routine: scheduling group work, tracking deadlines, borrowing shared supplies, communicating about a club event, dividing project roles, or another teacher-approved routine.

Your Five-Question Interview Guide

  1. Tell me about the last time you [experienced this routine].
  2. What did you do first?
  3. Where did the process slow down or become difficult?
  4. How did you handle that difficulty?
  5. What mattered most to you in that situation, and why?

Interview three classmates for about four minutes each. Use A, B, and C instead of names. Ask the same core questions, listen more than you speak, and do not promise a solution. Ask permission before recording an exact phrase. Anyone may skip a question or stop.
